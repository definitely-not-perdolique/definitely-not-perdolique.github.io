Word order is the only thing we can rely on to understand relationships between words since endings and suffixes barely exist in English.
There are four types of senteces: declarative, iterrogative, imperative and exclamatory, with declarative being the most common.
Therefore declarative word order is the basis for all other types. The minimum requirement for a sentence is SUBJECT + PREDICATE. But the most classic word order is SUBJECT + PREDICATE + OBJECT, also conveniently called SVO (because the predicate usually is a verb).

Let's break down this sentence: Mike read an interesting story yesterday
It's SUBJECT + PREDICATE + ATTRIBUTE + OBJECT + ADVERB, I assume. Though adverb isn't even a part of sentence, it's a part of speech. "Yesterday" would be ADVERBIAL MODIFIER then.

Word order often depends on type of a verb. Transitive verbs require a direct object e.g. Tom opens the door. Intrasitive do not. Tom coughs.
Some transitive verbs (give, send, tell) are followed by two objects direct and non direct. Tom sent the refrigerator an apple. The apple being the direct object.

Linking verbs (be, become, feel get grow, look, seen) are usually followed by nouns or adjectives.

Following are brief formulas to be expanded in next chapters:

Declarative sentences:
SUBJECT + PREDICATE + (OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Tom writes short stories for children.

Interrogative sentences:
General questions -
AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Does Anne have an apple today? - Yes she does.

Special questions -
QUESTION WORD + AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Why does Anne know the answer in the first place? - Because.

Alternative questions
It's questions with options, word order before "or" is the same as in general questions.
Which is AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB + OBJECT or...
Do you know his name or do you not? - His name is Tom.

Tag questions -
The first part is a statement, and the second is a short question, separated by a comma.
Tom likes apples, doesn't he? - No, he doesn't.

Imperative sentences
The same word order as in statements, but the subject, which is you, is usually omitted.
Don't you cry.

Requests
Requests are usually mimic general questions, but are using could, may, will, would as auxiliary verbs. And word "please" randomly appears.
COULD + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Could you stay calm, please.
Would you mind helping me with this report?

Exclamatory sentences
Exactly the same as statements!
Sometimes the subject and the linking verb are omitted, though.
What a beautiful present!

Simple, compound, and complex sentences
Clause is a group of words containing a subject and a predicate. Indepented clause can be a separate sentence, but a dependent clause can not.

Simple sentences consist of one independent clause.
Life goes on

Compund senteces consist of two or more independent caluses connected by a coodrinating conjunction (and, but, or).
Tom hates apples, and Anne thinks the moon is cheese.
Instead of "and" we can use semicolon, but it doesn't look any good in my opinion.

Complex sentences consist of the main clause and subordinate clause connected by subordinating conjuctions like "that", "after", "when", "since"...
I told him that the moon is a wheel of cheese.

Word order in commands
There are a lot of types of commands: direct orders, vague instructions, unsolicited advice, superfluous warnings, impossible requests.
Word order is the same as in declarative sentences, but the subject is omitted. This vacuum creates a sense of intention.

Declarative form would be:
You call me at five o'clock. You see how cringe and autistic it sounds.

To be polite you should add a taglike question.
Bring me that report, would you? – Sure.
Won't you please be quiet? – Sorry.
The last sentence doesn't sound quite polite and doesn't even look like a command. How is this different from a question?
Ok, polite requests are expressed in the form of general questions with the help of "could, may, will, would".

In negative commands "you" could emerge for the sake of emphasis.
Don't do that again / Don't you ever do that again.
The word order becomes weird though. The subject is placed after the predicate. They call "don't" a negative operator here. Because it establishes the meaning of the whole sentence as negative and we barely care what happens after that.

There are also a wide variety of signs like "Follow this sign", "Walk", "Stop", "Warning!", "Danger!".
The simpler the better...

Often requests are asked in the form of general questions:
Will you please sit down? - Thank you
Would you mind waiting a little? – OK.
There is a rule that requests usually dont use negative questions so the "won't you please shut up" construction doesn't belong to the requests category and is a command.

Asking for permisiion is also a request and not a command.

In this sentence the speaker went completely insane from politeness and used imagery tense for a question. Hence "closed" instead of close.

Agreement of subject and predicate
The verb (that is, the verb in the predicate) agrees with the subject in person and number.
I work. My brother works. My brothers work.
In the past tense, however, main verbs use the same verb forms for all persons.
I knew. He knew. They knew.
The verb BE is much more flexible in expression of agreement with the subject in person and number.
I am. You were. He is. etc

The subject in the singular requires the verb in the singular. The subject in the plural requires the verb in the plural
His daughter likes cartoons. She likes cartoons. - His daughter like cartoon. They like cartoons.
It's important to make the verb to agree with the subject and not with the random noun in the predicate or in the attributive phrase.
The can of olives is on the kitchen table. The cans of olives are on the kitchen table.
The choice of the singular or plural form of the verb depends not only on the form of the subject but also on the meaning of the subject. So the subject that is plural in form but singular in meaning takes a singular verb and vice versa.
Physics is cool. People are cool too.

Compound subject
If two subjects are connected with "and" a plural verb is used.
The dictionary and the grammar book are on the writing desk.
If the subject connected by "and" refer to the same person or thing, the singular form of the verb is used
Her friend and adviser accompanies her to important meetings.
If the subject are regarded as a unit then it's the singular verb again.
Strawberries and cream is my favorite dessert.

If the subjects are connected by "or; either...or; neither...nor", the verb agrees in number with the nearest subject.
Either the suspect or the witnesses are lying.
If you have a choice it's better to place the plural subject closer to the verb and use plural form.

If the compound subject stand after the predicate expressed by "there is; there are" the verb usually agrees in number with the nearest subject.
There is a book, a newspaper, and two magazines on the table.
Though some style manuals say that plural form is also ok. Like There are a hawk, an eagle, a dove...
Once again, if you have a choice it's better to place plural subject closer to the "there are" construction and use plural.
There are two magazines, a book, and a newspaper on the table.

Note_1
The phrases "accompanied by; as well as; along with; together with; including" following the subject don't change the number of the subject. Basically you can completely ignore them.
*The actress, accompanies by friends and fans, is travelling in Asia now.*
*The players, as well as the coach, are down with the flu.*
Note_2
The titles of books, movies etc are treated as a singular unit.
*Romeo and Juliet is a play by Shakespeare. (but Romeo and Juliet are Italians)*

Collective nouns
Some collective nouns are generally used with a singular verb
*At that time the bourgeoisie was mostly excluded from active political life*
Others with a plural verb
*The police are looking for the robbers.*
And some collective nouns (family, team, committee, crew, public, board, jury, staf...) take a singular or plural verb depending on the meaning.
If the group of people is regarded as unit then the verb also is singular. If the members of a group are regarded separately, the plural form of the verb is used.
*Her family is rich.*
*Her family are discussing this problem now.*
*The committee has adopted the plan*
*The committee have different opinions on this issue*
To convey the meaning of separate members it's better to add the word "members" itself.
*The committee members have different opinions on this issue.*

There are nouns which ending in s/es but are singular in meaning.
*The latest news is not very encouraging*
*Mathematics has always been a difficult subject for me.*
Names of countries, organizations and companies are singular in meaning too, despite often having plural endings.
*The United States is a large country. God bless America.*
*The United Nations was formed in 1945.*
A lot of tricky words like sheep, deer, fish, money, aircraft exist. So you can check the "Irregular Plural Nouns" section for further clarification.
