Nouns
There are a lot of noun classes, but the most problematic division for me is the one between countable and uncountable. Too often it seems to be completely arbitrary.
Gender of nouns is dictated solely by context and meaning. Animals are usually "it", though.
Number of nouns is simple, but there is a whole bunch of irregular plural nouns like "man - men" which have to be memorized.
Then animate nouns aka living things can show possession (притяжательный падеж) with an apostrophe ('s) and inanimate ones are more likely to use the preposition "of". And nouns dont have any special endings to indicate their case (case = падеж), so they rely on the word order, in the sense that the first noun is the subject and the second one is the object. Like the cat hates the dog and not the dog hates the cat.
There are suffixes that mark nouns, but they arent really reliable, so who cares. The better thing to do is to deduce the function of the word and then decide whether it's a noun or an adjective.

Adjectives are known for forming the degrees of comparison with suffixes i.e. big, bigger, the biggest. Or by using the words "more", "most", when the number of syllables exceeds two.
Just like nouns adjectives are the most reliably identified by their function in the sentence. Usually it's an attribute.

Adverbs (Наречия)
It's like adjectives but not really as far as I remember from school. Often they have the suffix -ly, but some of them are just simple words like "never", "ever", "here", or "there".
So, here's the main definition, listen closely. The main difference between an adjective and an adverb is that the former describes a noun and the latter describes a verb. Hence "-jective" (a noun is usually a subject or an object) and "-verb", I wildly guess.
They also stick to the certain themes like manner, time, frequency, place or direction in their describing of verbs.

Numerals can be cardinal or ordinal. Let me guess, ordinal is the place in a row, like "seventh" or "first". And then cardinal means quantity, like "seven apples" or "one rose".
"Cardinal" is a somewhat confusing name compared to "ordinal". But it's easier to remember when you understand that quantity is the general function of numbers.
Ordinals almost always are used with definite article, so it's "The Seventh" and "The First" actually.

Conjunctions (союзы) can be coordinating, meaning they connect parallel elements, single words, or whole clauses. Talking about "and" and "but". But conjunctions can also be subordinating. These are more meaningful "after", "if" or even "because", connecting subodinate clauses with the main clause.

Interjections (междометия)
It's like "alright", "yeah yeah yeah". Give me some synthesizer, Zac! Ok, its your part coming on. Are you ready? Right now, one, two, three. It' fun to stay at Young Men's Christian Association. So fun to stay at Young Men's Christian Association. ♫

Verbs
Main verbs are principal verbs or notional verbs (смысловой глагол). Like "work" or "live". They change their form in the third person singular by adding -s. e.g. "it lives".
Auxiliary (ауксиларии) verbs serve all kinds of functions. Mostly forming tenses with "have", "will", etc. "Be" can also be a linking verb. And "do" form questions.

Modal verbs are also called "defective verbs" because they can't form tenses in the regular way and basically don't exist without a main verb. Usually they express attitude towards the main action like not just "swim" but "must swim" or "may drown".

Then there's long list of irregular verb to memorize.

All verb have four forms: The Infinitive (to), The Gerund (-ing, noun), The Present Participle (-ing, attribute, forming continuous tense) and The Past Participle (-ed, attribute, forming passive and perfect)

Prepositions stand before a noun and postpositions after. There are very few true pospositions: ago, aside, apart, long. You can also move half of a phrasal verb like "up" in "wake up" to the end of the sentence and call it postposition, but it's actually a particle.
Prepositions are far more common: to, into... there are whole categories of them. Prepositions of place (on, in,at), of direction (to, trough), of time (at, for), of manner (by, with) etc

Word order is the only thing we can rely on to understand relationships between words since endings and suffixes barely exist in English.
There are four types of senteces: declarative, iterrogative, imperative and exclamatory, with declarative being the most common.
Therefore declarative word order is the basis for all other types. The minimum requirement for a sentence is SUBJECT + PREDICATE. But the most classic word order is SUBJECT + PREDICATE + OBJECT, also conveniently called SVO (because the predicate usually is a verb).

Let's break down this sentence: Mike read an interesting story yesterday
It's SUBJECT + PREDICATE + ATTRIBUTE + OBJECT + ADVERB, I assume. Though adverb isn't even a part of sentence, it's a part of speech. "Yesterday" would be ADVERBIAL MODIFIER then.

Word order often depends on type of a verb. Transitive verbs require a direct object e.g. Tom opens the door. Intrasitive do not. Tom coughs.
Some transitive verbs (give, send, tell) are followed by two objects direct and non direct. Tom sent the refrigerator an apple. The apple being the direct object.

Linking verbs (be, become, feel get grow, look, seen) are usually followed by nouns or adjectives.

Following are brief formulas to be expanded in next chapters:

Declarative sentences:
SUBJECT + PREDICATE + (OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Tom writes short stories for children.

Interrogative sentences:
General questions -
AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Does Anne have an apple today? - Yes she does.

Special questions -
QUESTION WORD + AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Why does Anne know the answer in the first place? - Because.

Alternative questions
It's questions with options, word order before "or" is the same as in general questions.
Which is AUXILIARY VERB + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB + OBJECT or...
Do you know his name or do you not? - His name is Tom.

Tag questions -
The first part is a statement, and the second is a short question, separated by a comma.
Tom likes apples, doesn't he? - No, he doesn't.

Imperative sentences
The same word order as in statements, but the subject, which is you, is usually omitted.
Don't you cry.

Requests
Requests are usually mimic general questions, but are using could, may, will, would as auxiliary verbs. And word "please" randomly appears.
COULD + SUBJECT + MAIN VERB (+ OBJECT + ADVERBIAL MODIFIER)
Could you stay calm, please.
Would you mind helping me with this report?

Exclamatory sentences
Exactly the same as statements!
Sometimes the subject and the linking verb are omitted, though.
What a beautiful present!

Simple, compound, and complex sentences
Clause is a group of words containing a subject and a predicate. Indepented clause can be a separate sentence, but a dependent clause can not.

Simple sentences consist of one independent clause.
Life goes on

Compund senteces consist of two or more independent caluses connected by a coodrinating conjunction (and, but, or).
Tom hates apples, and Anne thinks the moon is cheese.
Instead of "and" we can use semicolon, but it doesn't look any good in my opinion.

Complex sentences consist of the main clause and subordinate clause connected by subordinating conjuctions like "that", "after", "when", "since"...
I told him that the moon is a wheel of cheese.

Also, an important thing about modal verbs that I forgot to mention the last time. After modals "to" is dropped in infinitives. "You need go", not "need to go". Wrong! "Need to go" is correct here, because "need" isn't a modal verb for whatever reason. Just forget that need is modal. You can go now. It also called "bare infinitives", by the way, I mean infinitives without "to". And "need" is called semi-modal, marginal modal, not-always-modal.
